# Uranium Glass Jewelry FAQs

## What is uranium glass?

Uranium glass is glass made with uranium dioxide for color. The uranium dioxide content causes the glass to fluoresce bright green under a UV blacklight. Uranium dioxide was, and still is, added to glass because it produces brilliant, beautiful colors unmatched by any other glassmaking method. It comes in a huge variety of colors, especially in jewelry.

## Is uranium glass radioactive?

Yes, technically uranium glass is radioactive, however, the radiation is extremely minimal. Most pieces of uranium glass jewelry will have no measurable radiation above background levels.

![](/library-media/image017.jpg)

Uranium glass brooch with a very small radiation reading. Above 50cpm is considered a positive reading. For comparison, a Fiestaware bowl that uses uranium glaze can read up to 7,000cpm and the radium paint on the hands of an antique clock can measure up to 150,000cpm. I have measured countless pieces of uranium glass jewelry and beads and I have never gotten a reading above 300cpm.

## Is wearing uranium glass safe?

No. It's not safe. You need to send it to me. I am a trained professional.

Just kidding. It's perfectly safe. A piece of uranium glass jewelry will barely exceed background radiation levels if it does at all. You get exposed to more radiation from flying in a plane, hiking in a canyon with a lot of granite, fertilizing your garden, or even eating a banana. Also, uranium is primarily an alpha radiation emitter, which cannot pass through skin.

Here are some sources with good info on the safety of uranium glass -

"Glass: glassware, especially antique glassware with a yellow or greenish color, can contain easily detectable quantities of uranium. Such uranium-containing glass is often referred to as canary or vaseline glass. (Source: Study by Prof. Dr. Gerhard Billek, Hamburg - https://www.uranglas.ch/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Strahlenbelastung_Uranglas-1.pdf)

Drinking out of this glass (their math was done at 10% presence of uranium) and storing items in them, gives a max exposure per person of "this individual could receive an annual EDE of about 1.8×10-3 mrem [that's 0.0018 mrem] from ingestion of uranium leached from glassware." That's exclusively drinking out of these glasses (290 L/yr) and storing drinks in containers as well. And that is less than two-thousandths of a single mrem. ## Is uranium glass more dangerous if it is broken? Does breaking the glass release the radiation?

No, uranium glass is not more dangerous if broken. The uranium dioxide is in the glass at the molecular level. It is just as much a part of the glass is the melted silica sand that makes up the majority of the glass. It cannot be separated once it is added to the molten glass, and no extra radiation is released if it is broken.

With that said, safety precautions still need to be on place if you are cutting or grinding uranium glass. Glass dust is dangerous to inhale whether it is uranium or not. An N95 mask is required for cutting glass, and ideally a water system and/or some kind of ventilation.

Uranium glass is identified with a UV blacklight, and it's only uranium glass if the item is truly glass, not another material, and fluoresces the telltale bright neon green. If a piece of glass glows bright green in UV light then it contains uranium. No other material or element in glass has that effect. Because of this, uranium glass cannot be faked. Even if it could, it's not cost-effective to do so - uranium glass is cheap and plentiful. All of my beads are high-quality Czech uranium glass. Other jewelry components such as resin, plasic, enamel, paint, acrylic, powder coating, glow-in-the-dark materials, some gemstones, and even adhesive can fluoresce bright green like uranium, so it is imperative for a collector and seller of such glass in jewelry to make sure the glowing item is truly glass.

These pieces are glowing because of manganese, not uranium. Manganese was primarily used as a glass clarifier, which is why much of it is clear. Uranium glass will never be clear because uranium dioxide was used as a colorant.

## Should I use a Geiger counter to identify uranium glass?

It is not recommended to use a Geiger counter to identify uranium glass for several reasons -

Uranium is primarily an alpha particle emitter, and household Geiger counters do not measure alpha radiation

Lots and lots of uranium glass does not have enough radioactivity to measure above background radiation levels

Many uranium glass pieces in jewelry are too small to be measured

Now with that said, uranium glass does emit beta radiation, so a Geiger *can* be a useful tool when trying to determine if something is uranium glass if the glow is unusual. To be considered a positive identification for uranium glass, it must read 50cpm or above on a Geiger, which is considered above normal background radiation. If you test a piece and don't get a reading, that still does not mean the piece isn't uranium glass. For the above list of reasons, a Geiger counter can positively identify a piece of uranium glass, but it cannot negatively identify it.

## My background radiation reads about 20cpm on a Geiger, and then reads a piece of jewelry as 40cpm. Is it uranium glass?

To have a true positive reading for uranium content, an above normal amount of radiation must be detected. “Above normal” means higher than regular background radiation. Anything below 50cpm is still considered a "normal" amount of radiation, even if it reads higher than your regular background reading. It's when it hits above 50cpm that it's considered an above normal amount. A similar example would be if you get your cholesterol measured - anything less than 200 is considered normal cholesterol. If yours is 198, it's on the higher end of normal, but it's still not considered high cholesterol.

This does not mean the item is not uranium glass if it reads below 50cpm. Lots of uranium glass does not have enough radioactivity to read above background radiation. If the item is glass, and it glows *bright* green, then it contains uranium. Even some less vibrant glowing glass like dark blue can still be uranium. A Geiger can be a useful tool to positively identify uranium in pieces with an unusual glow, and as a fun tool in general, but it cannot be used to determine that something *is not* uranium glass.

## I have a piece of white uranium glass that doesn't seem to glow right. What gives?

White and cream color uranium glass is known as custard glass. While it generally glows fairly bright, it sometimes does not have the vivid green of other colors of uranium glass. Some pieces can have a softer green glow. Some pieces can look like they barely glow at all, and not very green whatsoever. Those can be difficult to pinpoint as uranium glass unless they can be measured on a Geiger counter. Custard glass of this kind is not very common.

## How do I care for my uranium glass jewelry?

Glass is a more delicate material, so keeping that in mind when wearing it is best practice. The main concern with this is rings since they are knocked about easily. Despite this, I have had very few reports of glass in rings breaking out of the thousands I've sold. I find glass rings in perfect condition that are 100 or more years old, so it does hold up. With that said, I do offer free lifetime replacements for the glass gems in my pieces. Some gems are very rare and I cannot replace, but most of them I will be able to replace for years to come.

Glass jewelry cannot be cleaned in an ultrasonic machine. It will break. Likewise, my body jewelry cannot go into an autoclave. Soap and water and a small brush is best. You can also use rubbing alcohol.

The exception to this is if the glass stones have foil backing, in which case they cannot get wet. The best way to clean is to use rubbing alcohol on the tops of the gems, and then around the rest of the piece to get the dirt and grime off.

## How do I know if a gems or beads in a piece of jewelry are made of glass?

Glass will be cold to the touch as opposed to plastic or acrylic. It will also be heavier.

If you tap the item on your teeth, glass will make a “clink clink” noise, and plastic/acrylic will make a “click click” noise. If you are new to this, I recommend trying both together so you can get a feel for the difference. It's quite noticeable.

I recommend using the “pin test” as a very last resort. Glass will not scratch with a sharp pin, but acrylic and plastic will. If you need to do this, do it in an inconspicuous place that won't hurt the piece.

## How do I know if my piece is sterling silver or solid gold?

Many pieces are marked as “Sterling” or “925.” Sometimes they aren't marked at all but can still be sterling silver. Sometimes, but not very often, something can be marked as sterling but is not. If you want to be absolutely sure, I recommend a testing kit. This can also test for gold content as well.

https://a.co/d/fUXGejh

Sometimes pieces can be marked “850” or “800” which means they are still silver, but not sterling. They have a lower silver content.

Gold is not always marked, so it can be good to have a testing kit for this as well. Sometimes the gold mark will be wrong i.e. marked as 14k but tests as 10k. This is not common. Gold can also be marked as “750” or “585” which is their gold percentage. 750 is 75% gold, or 18k, 585 is 58.5% gold which is 14k. Pieces considered to be “solid gold” can go as low at 8k. 9k gold, especially if they are marked 9ct, tends to be British made. The testing kit does not test for these amounts - it only goes down to 10k.

If you use the acid test kit, take care to keep it off your skin because it can cause a chemical burn. The kit comes with a neutralized.

If a piece is marked something like “14k HGE” that means heavy gold plated, or “14k GP” means gold plated.

If a piece has a mark that looks like “1/20th 14k GF” it means gold-filled, which is a different type of gold plating, usually over costume metal. These pieces tend to be quite old.

Gold vermeil is a type of thick gold plating over sterling silver and is usually unmarked, but the piece tends to be heavy.

One problem with the gold acid test is that gold-plated jewelry can have a positive gold test. If you want to be very sure, you can have a jeweler look at it, especially one that has a gold testing machine. However, if a piece is marked with gold content and tests postitive, it's a safe bet that it is solid gold.

## Lots of things glow under a blacklight. How do I know it's not a fluorescent gems or mineral instead of uranium glass?

Lots of gems and minerals glow under a blacklight, but there are only two common cut and faceted gems found in jewelry that glow bright green, and that is uranium glass and lab spinel.

UV reactive spinel is a synthetic/lab-created gemstone that looks like and glows bright green just like uranium glass. Lab spinels that are yellow, green, aqua, paraiba blue, and dark teal are the ones that fluoresce green. They have the advantage of looking just like uranium glass, but they are much more durable. I have been selling these alongside my uranium glass jewelry for years and they are very popular. They also tend to be worth a little bit more than uranium glass. They are extremely beautiful in natural light.

![](/library-media/image069.jpg)

UV reactive lab spinel gemstone rings

Pictured in costume metal, sterling silver, and solid gold

If a faceted green gem glows bright neon green, it will be either uranium glass or spinel 99% of the time.

UV reactive minerals such as willemite, yooperlite, and sodalite will not be found as faceted gems, and if found in jewelry at all, will look like chunks of rocks. These also usually require a 254nm short wave blacklight to glow.

![](/library-media/image070.jpg)

Willemite mineral specimen glowing under a 254nm short wave blacklight

Diamonds

Natural diamonds that are formed near uranium deposits can glow bright green. It's also not uncommon to find natural diamonds that fluoresce bright blue. Lab-grown diamonds will not fluoresce. Fluorescence does not affect the value of the diamond.

Diamonds irradiated to intensify the color can also glow green. This is especially true for canary diamonds.

## How do I know if them gem(s) in my piece are glass or spinel?

Aside from a presidium tester, there are several easy ways to identify which is which -

1. Jewelry items that have multiple small gems are going to be rhinestones. Rhinestones are made of glass. Spinel jewelry almost always has just one larger gem.

2. This one is really important - green spinels are a very warm lime green color, and uranium glass is a cool tone peridot color.

3. Spinels are always found in gold and sterling silver. Spinels will not be found in costume metal jewelry. Costume metal jewelry will have glass. However, uranium glass can be found in gold and silver also. If you have a gold/silver piece and aren't sure, you can take it to any jeweler who can test it for you. Just make sure they actually use a presidium and not just their eyeballs because it's easy to get it wrong, and most jewelers do not know what spinel is. A presidium can at least identify it as glass vs. gemstone. I'm also always happy to answer identifying questions! I can usually tell with good pics.

4. Spinels tend to be much larger gems with a lot more and fancier faceting. This isn't always the case but it can be a clue. I personally have never seen a spinel smaller than 6mm, and even that is very small for a spinel.

![](/library-media/image082.jpg)

Spinel gemstone in an unprotected mount

5. If the gem is in an unprotected setting, meaning the edges of the gem stand out where they can be knocked, unprotected by the mount, then it's more likely spinel. This is not a hard and fast rule but just another clue.

6. Because spinels are hard stones, they don't get chipped and scratched as easily. If the gem has a lot of wear or damage, it's more likely glass. This is not a hard rule as spinels can still have wear over a hundred years, but one of the clues you can look at.

Synthetic spinels have been made since the late 1800s, but started being manufactured in larger quantities during the 1920s. Not a lot of sellers know what a spinel is, and they are very often misidentified as other gemstones. They are also sometimes mislabeled as uranium glass, however, word about them has spread quickly (thanks to yours truly 😁) and it appears this is happening less as the years go by.

I always recommend spinels for engagement/wedding rings for uranium collectors, and anyone that wants a uranium glass ring for daily wear, due to their durability. They glow just as bright and as green as uranium glass under both 365nm and 395nm.
